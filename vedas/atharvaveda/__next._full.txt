1d:T80e,The Atharvaveda is the fourth and most distinct of the four Vedas. Named after the ancient sage Atharvan who is credited with discovering fire and establishing the earliest ritual practices, the Atharvaveda represents a unique branch of Vedic knowledge that addresses the practical concerns of everyday life alongside profound philosophical speculation. The name 'Atharvaveda' combines 'Atharvan', referring to the sage and his tradition of sacred fire practices, with 'Veda', meaning knowledge. An alternative name for this text is the Atharvangirasa Veda, acknowledging both the sage Atharvan and the sage Angiras whose hymns also form a significant part of the collection. While the Rigveda focuses on hymns of praise, the Yajurveda on sacrificial formulas and the Samaveda on musical chants, the Atharvaveda addresses a remarkably wide range of human concerns. Its 730 hymns spread across 20 books cover topics including healing and medicine, protection from disease and evil, domestic ceremonies, marriage and funeral rites, royal consecration, agricultural practices, philosophical inquiry and cosmological speculation. The Atharvaveda was initially not included in the Trayi Vidya or the threefold sacred knowledge of the earlier Vedic tradition. Over time, however, its importance was recognized and it was accepted as the fourth Veda. The Brahma priest, who supervises the entire sacrificial ceremony and must possess knowledge of all the Vedas, draws particularly upon the Atharvaveda. The two main recensions of the Atharvaveda are the Shaunaka and the Paippalada. The Shaunaka recension is more widely studied and contains 730 hymns organized into 20 books. The Paippalada recension, preserved primarily in the Odisha and Kashmir traditions, contains additional material not found in the Shaunaka version. The Atharvaveda's unique character makes it an invaluable source for understanding the full breadth of Vedic civilization, from the spiritual heights of philosophical inquiry to the practical realities of health, agriculture and community life.1e:T7f6,The Atharvaveda contributes significantly to Indian philosophical thought, particularly in the areas of cosmology, the nature of time and the relationship between the individual and the universe. Several hymns in the Atharvaveda explore the mystery of creation in ways that complement and extend the cosmological speculations of the Rigveda. The Skambha Sukta or Hymn to the Cosmic Pillar describes a universal supporting principle that holds together all of creation. This cosmic pillar or Skambha is identified with Brahman, the ultimate reality, and the hymn asks probing questions about the nature of this foundational support. The concept of Kala or Time receives extensive treatment in the Atharvaveda. Time is described as a cosmic force that creates and dissolves all things, that drives the seasons and sustains the rhythm of life. This philosophical exploration of time as a divine principle influenced later Hindu, Buddhist and Jain philosophical thinking. The Atharvaveda also develops the concept of Brahman more explicitly than the earlier Vedas. Several hymns describe Brahman as the supreme reality underlying all existence, the source from which everything emerges and to which everything returns. These teachings anticipate the systematic Brahman philosophy of the Upanishads. The Mundaka Upanishad, associated with the Atharvaveda, introduces the important distinction between Para Vidya, higher knowledge leading to the realization of Brahman, and Apara Vidya, lower knowledge including the study of the Vedas themselves. This teaching emphasizes that true spiritual liberation transcends even scriptural learning and requires direct experiential knowledge of ultimate reality. The Prashna Upanishad, also connected to the Atharvaveda, explores six fundamental questions about the nature of creation, the life force, the mind and the relation of the individual to the universal. Together, these Upanishads establish the Atharvaveda as a significant source of the deepest philosophical teachings of the Vedic tradition.1f:T476,The Atharvaveda is widely regarded as the foundation of the ancient Indian medical tradition that later developed into Ayurveda. Many hymns describe the properties of medicinal plants, the causes and treatments of diseases, and prayers for health and longevity. The text identifies numerous healing herbs and describes their applications for treating various ailments. Hymns address specific conditions including fever, cough, skin diseases, wounds, poisoning and mental afflictions. The treatments combine herbal remedies with sacred mantras and ritual practices, reflecting the Vedic understanding that health involves the harmony of body, mind and spirit. One famous hymn describes the healing power of water as a universal medicine, while others praise specific plants for their ability to restore vitality and drive away disease. The Atharvaveda's approach to healing is holistic, recognizing that physical wellness cannot be separated from psychological and spiritual well-being. This integration of medical knowledge with spiritual practice established a tradition that continues to influence Indian healing systems to the present day.20:T4b3,A significant portion of the Atharvaveda is devoted to the rituals and concerns of ordinary household life. Unlike the grand sacrificial ceremonies described in the other Vedas, these hymns address the everyday needs of families and communities. The text contains hymns for marriage ceremonies, prayers for the welfare of children, protection of the home, success in agriculture and trade, and the resolution of disputes. There are mantras for safe travel, for finding lost property and for ensuring harmony within the family. Funeral hymns guide the proper performance of last rites and offer prayers for the peaceful journey of the departed soul. Birth ceremonies welcome new life with blessings for health, wisdom and prosperity. The Atharvaveda also includes hymns related to royal governance and statecraft. The Rajasūya and other royal consecration ceremonies draw upon Atharvavedic mantras, and there are hymns that describe the qualities of just kingship and the duties of rulers toward their people. These domestic and civic hymns reveal a culture deeply concerned with the well-being of individuals and communities, recognizing that spiritual life encompasses all aspects of human experience.21:T417,One of the most remarkable hymns in the Atharvaveda is the Prithvi Sukta or Hymn to the Earth found in Book 12. This extended hymn celebrates the Earth as a divine mother who sustains all living beings. The Prithvi Sukta expresses a profound ecological awareness, describing the Earth's mountains, rivers, forests, seasons and the interconnected web of life they support. The hymn prays for the Earth's continued fertility, for the well-being of all creatures that inhabit her surface, and for harmony between human civilization and the natural world. The hymn declares that the Earth bears many nations speaking many languages and following many customs, expressing an early vision of universal tolerance and coexistence. It recognizes the diversity of life as a manifestation of the Earth's abundance and creative power.
